     Row’s payoff = 2rc + (1 − r)(1 − c) = 2rc + 1 − r − c + rc

=== P99 (283 chars) ===

The expected payoff to the hawk type:
                             H = −2p + 4(1 − p).
The expected payoff to the dove type:
                             D = 2(1 − p).
=== P100 (252 chars) ===
If H>D,  the fraction of hawk types in the population increase, and if H<D, the number of dove types increase.
The only way the population can be in equilibrium is if the payoffs to each type are the same:
H = −2p + 4(1 − p) = 2(1 − p) = D,
or
p = 1/2.

Bargaining game
---
Rubinstein bargaining model
Alice and Bob, have $1 to divide between them. 
They agree to spend at most three days negotiating over the division.
The first day, Alice will make an offer.
      The next day, Bob either accepts or comes back with a counteroffer.
      The third day, Alice gets to make one final offer. 
      If they cannot reach an agreement in three days, both players get zero.

Assume Alice and Bob differ in their degree of impatience: Alice discounts payoffs in the future at a rate of α per day, and Bob discounts payoffs at a rate of β per day.
We also assume that if a player is indifferent between two offers, he will accept the one that is most preferred by his opponent. 
We start our analysis at the end of the game, right before the last day.
       At this point Alice can make a take-it-or-leave-it offer to Bob. 
       Clearly, the optimal thing for Alice to do at this point is to offer Bob the
       smallest possible amount that he would accept, which, by assumption, is zero
=== P126 (636 chars) ===
Bargaining
---
If the game lasts three days, Alice will get $1 and Bob would get zero (i.e., an arbitrarily small amount).
Go back to the previous move, Bob should realize that Alice can guarantee herself $1 on the next move by simply rejecting his offer. 
A dollar next period is worth α to Alice this period, Bob certainly prefers 1−α now to zero next period, so he should rationally offer α to Alice, which Alice will then accept. 
So if the game ends on the second move, Alice gets α and Bob gets 1 − α.
Move to the first day. At this point Alice similarly offers β(1 − α) to Bob. Bob finds this (just) acceptable and the game ends.

The Ultimatum Game
Economists test the Rubinstein bargaining model in the laboratory. Unfortunately, it doesn’t work out like that.
There are many proposed explanations for this. One view is that too small an offer violates social norms of behavior.
Another, not inconsistent view, is that receivers get some utility payoff from hurting the offerers, in retaliation for the small offer.
